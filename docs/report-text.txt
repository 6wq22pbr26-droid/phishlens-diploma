Project overview 
We are developing PhishLens, a browser extension for phishing risk assessment with explanations 
based on observable website indicators. In this report, we present our user research, review of 
related work, technology choices, requirements, architecture, interface prototype, evaluation plan 
and team responsibilities. 
Our project is at the analysis and design stage. We have defined the scope and prepared an interface 
mock-up. We will develop and evaluate the detection model, browser extension and supporting 
service during the implementation stage.

1 Proposal and scope 
Problem and audience 
Our target users are university students and other everyday internet users who encounter suspicious 
links. We investigate how a browser extension can present a phishing risk estimate together with 
reasons that users can understand. Our initial research focuses on adults who identify themselves as 
university students. 
Aim and objectives 
Our aim is to design and develop an explainable browser extension for real-time phishing risk 
assessment. We will investigate user needs, review related research, select reproducible data and 
URL features, compare baseline models, develop a Chromium extension and supporting API, and 
evaluate detection quality, runtime, reliability and usability. 
First working version 
Our first MVP covers active-page URL checking, a baseline model, local inference where feasible, 
a popup with risk and reasons, and a minimal FastAPI service. Later work includes DOM and 
domain features, model explanations, optional reputation checks, caching, settings, Kazakh, Russian 
and English localization, feedback storage and broader evaluation. 
We prioritize local processing and clear handling of uncertainty. An unavailable analysis will 
appear as unavailable rather than as a low-risk result. Browsing history, form values, passwords, 
cookies and page content will not be uploaded by default. 
Expected deliverables 
Our final deliverables will include an installable extension, documented supporting components, 
reproducible data and model procedures, comparisons of alternatives and an evaluation report. For 
this probationary period, we present the requirements, design, research evidence and interactive 
interface mock-up. 
Contribution and novelty to investigate 
Our proposed contribution is a student-focused warning workflow with understandable reasons and 
local processing as the default. We will assess its value by comparing alternatives and evaluating 
both detection quality and user understanding.

Design decisions derived from this sample 
We selected Chromium for the first version (Q3, FR-06). We will use a brief result with expandable 
technical reasons because no warning format received a majority in Q7 (FR-03). We will make local 
checking the default and require consent for external checks (Q8, FR-05). We also plan Kazakh, 
Russian and English localization (Q10). Eight respondents expressed interest in trying the idea 
(Q11). 
Data quality and remaining evidence 
Q12 includes inconsistent selections: nine responses selected “none” and two selected “unsure”, 
while setup and incompatibility each received two selections. We therefore retain the aggregate 
counts without ranking the barriers. We will review the individual export before using this question 
to guide further decisions. 
Our user research currently consists of the questionnaire. We will extend it with user discussions or 
a substantive client consultation. Prototype usability testing is planned separately from this initial 
survey.

3 Literature review 
[S01] Lin Y. et al. (2021). Phishpedia: A Hybrid Deep Learning Based Approach to Visually 
Identify Phishing Webpages. USENIX Security, 3793–3810. 
Phishpedia identifies brand impersonation through logo recognition and domain comparison. This 
makes a brand mismatch explainable. A maintained reference set and visual inference are required; 
the published evaluation does not establish performance on our browser or local audience. We retain 
the idea of linking warnings to observable evidence, while excluding a full visual pipeline from the 
first MVP. 
[S02] Liu R. et al. (2022). Inferring Phishing Intention via Webpage Appearance and Dynamics: A 
Deep Vision Based Approach. USENIX Security, 1633–1650. 
PhishIntention combines apparent brand identity with credential-taking intent and page interaction. 
It shows why visual similarity alone may produce misleading alerts. Dynamic interaction adds cost 
and complexity. For PhishLens, later DOM analysis should examine structure without collecting 
form values or interacting with live phishing pages. 
[S03] Liu R. et al. (2023). Knowledge Expansion and Counterfactual Interaction for Reference-
Based Phishing Detection. USENIX Security, 4139–4156. 
DynaPhish expands reference knowledge and explores pages that lack familiar branding. This 
addresses coverage limits of fixed reference lists, but maintaining and validating the knowledge 
base becomes part of the system. Our evaluation should include unfamiliar brands; absence of a 
known logo cannot be treated as evidence of safety. 
[S04] Liu R. et al. (2024). Less Defined Knowledge and More True Alarms: Reference-based 
Phishing Detection without a Pre-defined Reference List. USENIX Security, 523–540. 
PhishLLM uses language-model knowledge and validation to reduce dependence on a predefined 
brand list. This offers broad brand coverage but introduces model reliability and external-service 
dependencies. We exclude an LLM from the mandatory local inference path and will express 
reasons from measured features instead of generating unverified accusations.

Literature review   robustness and explanations 
[S05] Li Y. et al. (2024). KnowPhish: Large Language Models Meet Multimodal Knowledge 
Graphs for Enhancing Reference-Based Phishing Detection. USENIX Security, 793–810. 
KnowPhish combines multimodal brand knowledge with textual and visual information, including 
cases where logos alone are insufficient. The knowledge infrastructure exceeds the first MVP. The 
transferable lesson is to evaluate coverage across site types and brands rather than assume one 
visual cue is universal. 
[S06] Ji F. et al. (2025). Evaluating the Effectiveness and Robustness of Visual Similarity-based 
Phishing Detection Models. USENIX Security, 3201–3220. 
The authors examine real-world effectiveness and robustness of visual similarity detectors, 
including evasion through visual changes. Their findings concern the evaluated visual approaches, 
not every phishing model. They motivate independent testing, distribution-shift checks and explicit 
error analysis rather than reporting a single favourable random-split score. 
[S07] Ribeiro M. T., Singh S., Guestrin C. (2016). “Why Should I Trust You?”: Explaining the 
Predictions of Any Classifier. KDD, 1135–1144. 
LIME approximates a model locally with an interpretable explanation. A local approximation can 
help inspect a prediction, but its stability and perturbation assumptions must be checked. We will 
compare explanation consistency and runtime; a model explanation is not proof that a website is 
malicious. 
[S08] Lundberg S. M., Lee S.-I. (2017). A Unified Approach to Interpreting Model Predictions. 
NeurIPS 30. 
SHAP assigns feature contributions to model predictions. This supports analysis of which URL 
features influence a score, subject to the model and explanation assumptions. Contributions do not 
establish causality. We will check whether a compact set of contributions can be translated 
faithfully into user-facing reasons within the latency budget. 
We use S01–S06 as the recent phishing-detection research base and S07–S08 as foundational 
explanation methods. These publications inform our design and evaluation; their results are not 
measurements of PhishLens.

Literature synthesis and data choice 
Synthesis 
The studies address brand impersonation, credential-taking intent, reference coverage and 
robustness. These approaches provide more context than URL-only classification, but require 
additional data, computation and maintenance. We therefore start with a reproducible URL baseline 
and reserve richer features for later development. We expect the URL-only baseline to miss some 
attacks that require page context. 
We will distinguish observed URL features from the model’s interpretation. A long or unusual URL 
may be an indicator, but does not establish malicious intent. We will evaluate SHAP and LIME for 
fidelity, stability and runtime, and test explanation quality separately from detection quality. 
Candidate data and leakage controls [S09] 
We selected the PhiUSIIL dataset as a candidate for our baseline [S09]. It contains 235,795 
instances and 54 features, including 134,850 legitimate and 100,945 phishing cases. Its original 
label is 1 for legitimate and 0 for phishing. We will use phishing_positive = 1 - label and retain the 
CC BY 4.0 attribution. We will separate URL features from features derived from page content. 
We will document the collection context, licence, label mapping and selected feature definitions. 
We will remove exact duplicates and group by registrable domain where feasible to reduce leakage 
across training and evaluation. We will fit preprocessing only on training data and keep a separate 
final test partition. We will also assess whether the available collection dates support a time-
separated evaluation. 
We will select features that are available during URL-only inference and exclude filenames, 
identifiers, labels and other columns that could leak the target. We will keep feature order and 
normalization consistent between training and inference, and record checksums, random seeds and 
dependency versions. 
Engineering sources [S10–S15] 
We use Safe Browsing, SmartScreen and Netcraft as engineering references for the system 
comparison and optional reputation checks. Chrome Manifest V3 documentation guides the 
extension lifecycle. We will test ONNX Runtime Web for browser inference and use FastAPI for 
validated API contracts [S10–S15].

Implications for PhishLens 
We intend PhishLens to complement browser protections through explanations of project-defined 
indicators, clear unavailable states and documented data handling. We will evaluate any 
performance advantage using a common labelled test protocol. Optional reputation checks will 
provide another signal; the absence of a match will remain inconclusive. 
Our research focuses on URL-feature explanations and evaluation with student users. We will keep 
browser-native protection enabled during testing and review Safe Browsing deployment terms 
before implementing a reputation integration.

6 Requirements 
FR-01 to FR-06 define the required behaviour of our first working version. Richer detection features 
remain outside the initial MVP. 
ID Requirement and acceptance condition 
FR-01 The extension will check the active page URL on supported navigation events and handle internal 
browser pages, missing URLs and invalid URLs without crashing. 
FR-02 Our analysis pipeline will extract reproducible features and apply a baseline model, with explicit 
risk, uncertain and unavailable results. 
FR-03 The popup will display the risk assessment and understandable reasons linked to observable 
indicators, including the relevant limitations. 
FR-04 Our system will define the extension and model input/output contract and provide a FastAPI service 
with validated schemas, documented responses and error handling. 
FR-05 Our design will keep browsing history off the server. Any optional external check will have a 
documented URL or domain payload and explicit consent. 
FR-06 The extension will support developer-mode installation from the repository, with installation, startup 
and safe demonstration instructions. 
Traceability from evidence 
We derived FR-03 from the warning preferences in Q7 and the explanation literature [S07–S08]. 
FR-05 reflects the local-processing preference in Q8. FR-06 follows the Chromium usage reported 
in Q3. FR-01 and FR-02 define the minimum analysis workflow. 
Safe behaviour for failures 
We will show explicit unsupported, unavailable or error states for invalid URLs, internal browser 
pages, missing models, invalid responses and timeouts. We will use inert examples for 
demonstrations and usability tasks.

Nonfunctional targets and acceptance 
ID Target and measurement 
NFR-01 Our proposed detection target is F1 ≥ 0.90 on held-out data. Evaluation will include precision, 
recall, ROC-AUC, class balance, error analysis and leakage controls. 
NFR-02 Our proposed local feature-extraction and inference target is ≤ 200 ms. Measurements will record 
browser, hardware and model version and separate local, service and total latency. 
NFR-03 Our interface will provide loading, unavailable, timeout and error states. We will evaluate 
reliability, usability and localization with safe examples. 
Acceptance measurement 
We will choose detection thresholds on validation data and freeze them before final testing. We will 
report confusion matrices and examples of false positives and false negatives, treating phishing as 
the positive class. Our proposed F1 target is at least 0.90. 
We will measure local feature extraction and inference over at least 100 safe-fixture checks. We will 
separate cold and warm runs and record the browser, hardware and model version. Our proposed 
latency criterion is p95 at or below 200 ms. We will measure API, reputation lookup and total user-
visible latency separately. 
We will test loading, result, unavailable and error states, keyboard interaction and explanation 
clarity. We will review Kazakh, Russian and English translations with speakers of those languages 
before evaluating localized interfaces. 
Privacy acceptance 
We will inspect network traffic in local-only mode to confirm that no URL, domain, page content, 
form value, password, cookie or browsing history leaves the device. Optional external checks will 
require consent and a documented payload. Any feedback storage will have defined fields, retention 
and deletion rules.

7 Architecture and interfaces 
Context and use cases 
We designed the extension to manage the active-page check, display results and perform local 
analysis where feasible. Our offline model pipeline will produce a versioned model and feature 
schema. An optional API and reputation path will be enabled separately and will have its own 
consent boundary. 
View Elements and relationships 
Context Student ↔ extension; model-build pipeline → versioned model/schema → extension; 
extension → optional consented API → later reputation provider. 
Use cases Check current page; read risk; expand reasons; handle unavailable result and retry; later 
change language and external-check consent. 
Components Popup ↔ event coordinator → URL feature extractor → model adapter → result/explanation 
mapper → popup. API adapter is an optional separate branch. 
Data boundary Default analysis remains local. No full page, form values, credentials or browsing history are 
sent. A later external check documents the exact selected URL/domain payload. 
Proposed result contract 
Our result contract includes status, risk category, modelVersion, featureSchemaVersion, reason 
codes and duration. A numerical score is optional and will be labelled as a model score unless we 
establish probability calibration. Error responses will include an error code and a retry indication. 
We define loading, analysed, unsupported, unavailable and error states. Reason codes will map to 
consistent interface text. We will render user-provided strings safely and maintain compatibility 
between model and feature-schema versions. 
Editable diagrams 
Our editable architecture files are context.mmd, use-cases.mmd and components.mmd, with 
corresponding SVG views. They are included in the tagged source package linked in Appendix D. 
We will test model export and extension runtime compatibility during implementation [S13–S14].

Warning hierarchy 
We place the risk category first, followed by an observable reason. A Details control provides 
technical context. The user can close the suspicious page independently. The interface labels 
demonstration data and future settings clearly. 
Survey connection 
We chose a layered interface because Q7 shows different preferences for detailed descriptions, 
scores and brief warnings. Q8 supports local processing as the default, while Q10 motivates later 
localization. We will evaluate the mock-up with users during the usability study. 
Interface verification 
We will check all four states, Details expansion, retry behaviour, keyboard focus and narrow-screen 
layout. We will also inspect network traffic. These checks assess the interface; detection quality and 
extension permissions will be tested during implementation.
